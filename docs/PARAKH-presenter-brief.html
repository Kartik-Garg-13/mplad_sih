01 — The 30-second answer

If a judge gives you one sentence

Say this

“MPLADS moves roughly ₹5 crore per MP per year through 769 district agencies. PARAKH reads the whole public record, flags the 28.6% of works that warrant a human look, and states its evidence and a plausible innocent explanation for every single one — because an AI that decides which MP is guilty is a defamation engine, not a tool.”

Then, if they want more, the three follow-on beats in order: what it reads (1,31,437 works and 1,09,006 payments, hand-pulled from the ministry's own citizen dashboard), what it does (13 detectors, 5 deterministic and 8 peer-relative statistical), and what it refuses to do (rank MPs by suspicion — enforced by a test in CI, not a promise in a slide).

The last beat is the one that wins the room. Every team at SIH can find anomalies. The differentiator is that PARAKH is built to be wrong safely.

02 — The problem & the trap

Why this problem statement is a trap

MPLADS gives every Member of Parliament an annual allocation for local infrastructure, spent through district implementing agencies. The records are public on eSAKSHI — but they are published as per-tile spreadsheet exports, one house at a time. Nobody can see the scheme as a whole, which means nobody can see the pattern in it.

So the naive build is obvious: score every MP for irregularity, rank them, ship the leaderboard. That system is a defamation engine. MPLADS works are implemented by district officers, not by MPs; an MP recommends, a District Authority sanctions and executes. A high anomaly count against a member's name is very often a statement about their district administration, their terrain, or their data-entry quality — and publishing it as a suspicion score is both wrong and legally reckless.

The problem statement itself warns about this. We treated that warning as the primary design constraint rather than a disclaimer to add at the end — which is why the restraint contract was written before the first detector.

The line to hold in the room

Every flag says “this warrants a look” — never “this is fraud.” If a judge pushes you to call something corrupt, that is the moment to hold the line, not soften it. The restraint is the product.

03 — What we built

A batch pipeline, not a live guess

Nothing in the served application computes a detector at request time. Raw exports are parsed into a canonical layer, every detector runs offline against that snapshot, and the results are written into a single read-only database the API only ever SELECTs from. A full rebuild takes well under a minute, and is deterministic.

One thing worth demoing that judges won't expect

PARAKH accepts new datasets at runtime. Upload another set of the same six exports — a later snapshot, a different house, a pilot district — and it joins the corpus alongside the built-in data, re-runs all 13 detectors against the merged whole, and becomes filterable as its own source. It refuses an upload whose work IDs already exist, because double-counting the same works would corrupt every peer-group statistic.

04 — The 13 detectors

Two tiers, never conflated

Tier A is a deterministic contradiction a human can verify by hand from the evidence sentence alone — arithmetic or logic, no statistics. Tier B is a statistical outlier against a named peer group, and abstains entirely below 30 peers rather than firing on noise. The tier is visible on the card, the list and the filter, always.

05 — How we validated it

Five methods, including the unflattering results

“How do you validate unsupervised anomaly detection with no labels?” is the hardest question in this problem statement, and it will be asked. We answer it five different ways and publish all five on the /validation page — including the two that came out badly. Volunteering a weak result before a judge finds it converts a vulnerability into a credibility win.

1. Synthetic injection — 182 constructed anomalies

Strong

Five known anomaly types injected into a copy of the real corpus, then run through the untouched detector suite. Recall is per-detector; “any” means some detector caught it.

duplicate_record → A3 100% · phantom_completion → A2 100% · agency_concentration → B6 100% · inflated_cost → B1 77.5% (82.5% any) · split_work → B5 57.1% (92.9% any)

2. Precision @ k — the ranking, not the detectors

Weak, and we say so

Of the top 50 and top 100 works in the default ranking, 0% are injected anomalies. The single-flag synthetic injections rank below real works that stack many flags from one legitimate batch-procurement pattern. This is a genuine limitation of ranking by flag count, and it is stated on the page rather than buried.

P@50 0.0% · P@100 0.0% · P@200 4.0% · P@500 2.8% · P@1000 2.5%

3. Bootstrap stability — does a detector agree with itself?

Mixed

Ten bootstrap resamples per detector, measuring Jaccard overlap of the flagged set against the baseline. Around 0.5–0.6 is reasonable for a resampled statistical detector. B7 at 0.056 is genuinely unstable — year-end bunching depends on a 95th-percentile cutoff across MPs that moves sharply under resampling. Know this number before a judge computes it.

B4 0.589 · B3 0.560 · B2 0.542 · B1 0.523 · B6 0.361 · B7 0.056 (min 0.0)

4. Known-case backtest — 0 of 2 matched

Structural miss

Two researched, named CAG/press cases were searched for in this corpus. Neither matched — and the reason is structural, not a search failure: CAG audits review works that are already years old, while this corpus is the 18th term, sanctioned within the last one to three years. One case's agency never appears in the corpus at all.

CAG Report No. 22 of 2025 → agency absent from corpus · press coverage ~2019–2022 → predates 18th-term coverage

5. Manual adjudication — every one of the top 50 reviewed by hand

Honest

All 50 top-ranked flagged works were reviewed manually and collapse to just 8 real batch-procurement programmes: 49 works benign-explained, 1 undecided, 0 plausibly irregular. This is the same finding as precision@k from the other side — the ranking surfaces large legitimate rollouts ahead of rarer single-instance anomalies. It is the most important honest result in the project.

50 works → 8 clusters · benign_explained 49 · undecided 1 · plausibly_irregular 0

Bonus: the stall-risk model, tested on the future

Solid

A LightGBM classifier predicts which currently-on-track works will stall, split by sanction date rather than randomly — trained on the past, tested on the future. Calibration is reported honestly: works scored ~87% actually stall about 67% of the time, and the calibration curve is drawn on the page showing exactly that gap.

52,417 train / 13,120 test · PR-AUC 0.577 vs 0.381 base rate · ROC-AUC 0.711 · 31,207 works scored
06 — The restraint contract

Seven rules, enforced by code

This is the differentiator. Each of these is a mechanism in the repository, not a principle in a slide — and rule 1 fails the build if violated.

01

Vocabulary lock

A test greps the entire codebase and all UI copy for a fixed list of accusatory words — fraud, corrupt, guilty, scam, siphon, embezzle — before every build. It has caught real violations during development and forced the copy to be rewritten, never allowlisted.

02

No flag without evidence

Every flag states its trigger, its peer group, the n of that group, and the observed value in one plain sentence — or it does not ship.

03

Benign explanations

All 13 detectors carry a curated, plausible innocent explanation, shown by default beside the evidence — not behind a tooltip or a click.

04

Confidence tier always visible

A or B appears on the card, in the list and in the filter. Deterministic and statistical findings are never presented as the same kind of thing.

05

No MP suspicion ranking

Works and agencies are ranked. Members of Parliament never are — anywhere in the product.

06

Provenance on every screen

Source, ingestion date, record counts, financial-year coverage and coverage percentages sit in the footer of every page, derived from the data itself so they cannot go stale.

07

Reviewer override

A human can mark a flag “reviewed — explained.” It is suppressed from the queue, never deleted, always reversible, and stored outside the rebuildable database so a refresh cannot wipe it.

08 — Hard questions

What judges will actually ask

Answer each of these in two sentences, then stop. The instinct to keep talking after a hard question is what loses the exchange.

“How do you validate this with no ground-truth labels?”

Five ways, and we publish all of them: synthetic injection of 182 constructed anomalies, precision@k, bootstrap stability, a known-case backtest against CAG and press reports, and manual adjudication of the entire top 50. Two of the five came out unflattering and are on the page anyway.

“28.6% of works flagged is far too many. Isn't that useless?”

Concede the premise, then reframe. It is too many to act on directly — and 12,681 of those flags are A5, works marked complete with no photo on file, which is largely a portal-era artifact rather than 12,681 problems.

The tier filter and the reviewer override exist precisely because triage is the job. Filter to Tier A and the queue is 9,199. This is a funnel for human reviewers, not a finished accusation list.

“Your manual review found zero irregularities in the top 50. Doesn't that mean it failed?”

It means the ranking underperforms, and we say that on the validation page in those words. The top 50 collapse into 8 legitimate batch-procurement programmes, because ranking by flag count rewards works that stack many flags from one benign pattern.

The detectors themselves score 77–100% recall on injected anomalies. The honest statement is: the detectors work, the default ranking needs a composite score, and that is named as the next piece of work rather than hidden.

“Why should I trust a statistical flag at all?”

You shouldn't trust it — you should check it, which is the entire design. Every Tier B flag names its peer group and the n of that group, abstains below 30 peers, and carries a plausible innocent explanation on the same card.

“What stops this from defaming an MP?”

Five things, all mechanical: MPs are never ranked; a CI test fails the build if accusatory vocabulary appears anywhere in the codebase or copy; every flag carries a benign explanation by default; the deterministic/statistical distinction is always visible; and a human reviewer can suppress any flag reversibly.

Structurally, MPs recommend and district authorities sanction and execute — so an MP-level suspicion score would be attributing district-administration behaviour to a member. That is why the ranking is on works and agencies.

“B7's stability is 0.056. Why is it still shipping?”

Because we would rather ship it with its instability published than quietly drop the inconvenient detector. Year-end bunching depends on a 95th-percentile cutoff across MPs that moves sharply under resampling, so the specific set of flagged works is not reproducible even though the underlying pattern is real and well-documented in public finance.

If pressed on what you'd do next: raise the eligibility threshold, or replace the percentile cutoff with an absolute share test.

“Two detectors find nothing. Why include them?”

A1 and A3 firing zero times is a result about the ministry's data quality, not a bug — the export is internally consistent on ledger arithmetic and free of duplicate work IDs. Both are fully implemented and both score 100% recall when the anomaly is synthetically injected, so they would fire on a dirtier dataset.

“Is this just a dashboard? Where's the AI?”

The statistical layer is peer-relative robust z-scoring with abstention, TF-IDF similarity clustering for work-splitting and near-duplicate detection, Louvain community detection on the agency graph, and a gradient-boosted stall-risk model evaluated on a forward time split.

The deliberate choice is that none of it runs at request time — it is a batch pipeline, so every number on screen is reproducible and auditable rather than a live model output nobody can re-derive.

“Can this scale to the whole scheme, or other schemes?”

It already ingests both houses at 1.3 lakh works, and a full rebuild is under a minute. New data can be added at runtime through the upload page without touching code — the parser was built so a new term or house is a directory, not a patch.

“What would you do with three more months?”

A composite ranking score to fix precision@k, which is the clearest weakness. Then Tier C — the three unshipped detectors — and a per-detector precision estimate from a larger hand-adjudicated sample.

09 — Known limits

Say these before you're asked

Each of these is already stated somewhere in the product. Volunteering them reads as rigour; being caught by them reads as overselling.

The default ranking underperforms. Precision@50 is 0%, and hand-adjudication of the top 50 found 49 benign and 0 plausibly irregular. A composite score is the named fix.

B7 is not reproducible. Bootstrap Jaccard of 0.056 means the specific flagged set shifts substantially under resampling.

Quantity coverage is 4.9%. B2 can only score cost-per-unit where a quantity is extractable from free text, and MPLADS descriptions are overwhelmingly location narratives — “from X's house to Y's house” — not specifications.

Photo evidence exists for only 23.8% of works, which is why A5 is the highest-volume detector and why it should be read as a coverage statistic as much as an anomaly.

The known-case backtest matched nothing, for the structural reason that CAG audits lag this corpus by years.

Three planned Tier C detectors were not shipped — 13 of 16. The methodology page names them rather than quietly reducing the count.

No constituency map and no per-capita normalisation. No boundary GeoJSON and no population dataset were ever sourced, so both were left out rather than faked.

Data is hand-downloaded, not scraped. Provenance is honest and reproducible, but a refresh is a manual pull from eSAKSHI's export buttons.
